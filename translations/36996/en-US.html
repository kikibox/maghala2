Rainfed barley yield per hectare: Strategies for increasing performance

Rainfed agriculture is considered the backbone of crop production in many arid and semi-arid regions around the world, including Iran. Among these, barley (Hordeum vulgare L.) plays a vital role in ensuring food security, livestock feed, and industrial uses due to its relative drought resistance and high adaptability to diverse climatic conditions. Rainfed barley yield per hectare is one of the most important indicators for assessing performance and profitability in rainfed farms and is always of interest to farmers, researchers, and policymakers. Achieving maximum yield potential in this type of cultivation depends not only on natural phenomena such as rainfall but also requires a deep understanding of the complex environmental, genetic, and management factors that affect the growth cycle of the barley plant.

Under current conditions characterized by climate change, severe fluctuations in rainfall patterns, and rising temperatures, optimizing rainfed barley yield per hectare has become even more important. This article provides a comprehensive review of these factors and presents novel strategies for increasing tonnage and improving the sustainability of rainfed barley production. It also discusses and analyzes the role of advanced irrigation technologies, including drip irrigation, in improving efficiency even in rainfed systems. According to a report by the United Nations Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO), improving the performance of rainfed crops, including barley, is a key strategy for addressing food security challenges and reducing poverty in drought-prone areas with growing populations.

The importance of rainfed barley cultivation in Iran and global agriculture: A pillar of food security

Barley, after wheat and rice, is the third most cultivated cereal in the world. Due to its diverse uses and ability to adapt to adverse environments, it is grown in rainfed agricultural systems across wide areas of Asia, Africa, and Europe. In Iran, rainfed barley cultivation accounts for a significant portion of agricultural land and plays a vital role in supplying livestock and poultry feed, producing malt for the beverage industry, and preparing local breads and food products. Given the country's limited water resources and increasing demand for agricultural products, increasing rainfed barley yield per hectare has become a national priority.

Rainfed barley cultivation allows farmers to produce in areas where conventional irrigation is not available or is economically unviable. This feature has made barley a strategic crop for smallholder and subsistence farmers. Barley's high resistance to environmental stresses such as drought, soil salinity, and low temperatures makes it a preferred option for dealing with climatic uncertainties. However, the yield potential of rainfed barley is directly affected by rainfall fluctuations, making precise management and the adoption of smart strategies essential for increasing sustainability and rainfed barley yield per hectare. In recent years, advances in science and technology have opened new avenues for increasing efficiency in these agricultural systems through the use of improved cultivars, improved tillage practices, and even limited supplementary irrigation during critical growth stages. Therefore, a precise understanding of the influencing factors and the application of novel strategies will be the key to achieving sustainable development in rainfed barley cultivation.

Key factors affecting rainfed barley yield per hectare: A comprehensive analysis

Rainfed barley yield per hectare, as a complex variable, is influenced by the interaction of multiple factors whose proper understanding and management can significantly impact final performance. These factors can be divided into four main categories: climatic, soil, genetic (cultivar), and management.

1. Climatic and weather conditions: The main axis in rainfed cultivation

Climate is undoubtedly the most important determining factor for the success or failure of rainfed cultivation. Fluctuations in weather patterns, especially in arid and semi-arid regions, have a direct impact on rainfed barley yield per hectare.

  • Rainfall amount and distribution: Rainfall is the most critical water source for dryland barley. Barley typically requires a minimum of 300 to 450 millimeters of rainfall during the growing season to complete its life cycle. However, the distribution of rainfall is as important as the total amount. Spring rainfall, particularly during the sensitive flowering and grain-filling stages, plays a vital role in determining final yield. Drought stress during these critical stages can significantly reduce 'dryland barley yield per hectare,' as the plant has its highest water requirements during this period. Regions with below-optimal rainfall or prolonged dry spells during the growing season experience substantially lower yields. More accurate weather forecasting and the use of rainwater harvesting and storage methods can help mitigate the negative effects of rainfall variability.
  • Temperature and Light Intensity: Optimal temperatures for various growth stages of barley range from 15 to 25 degrees Celsius. Very low temperatures in winter, especially in cold regions, can cause frost damage to the plant, reducing vigor and ultimately lowering 'dryland barley yield per hectare.' Conversely, late spring frosts are also dangerous for young barley. In the late growth stages, high temperatures and severe heat lead to a shortened grain-filling period and a reduced thousand-grain weight, which directly affects final tonnage. Sufficient light intensity is also vital for photosynthesis and biomass production; fields that receive adequate light have higher yield potential.
  • Drought and Heat Stresses: Dryland barley is constantly exposed to drought and heat stresses. These stresses can cause stomatal closure, reduced photosynthesis, impaired nutrient absorption, and ultimately decreased growth and yield. Selecting drought-resistant varieties and using soil management methods that help retain moisture (such as conservation tillage, mulching, and adding organic matter to the soil) can minimize the damaging effects of these stresses and help stabilize 'dryland barley yield per hectare.'

2. Soil Type and Fertility: The Foundation for Sustainable Growth

Soil is the growth medium and the primary source of water and nutrients for barley plants. The physical, chemical, and biological properties of soil directly impact 'dryland barley yield per hectare.'

  • Soil Texture and Structure: Medium-textured soils (loams) with a balanced ratio of sand, silt, and clay are considered ideal for dryland barley cultivation. These soils have good drainage and high water-holding capacity. Heavy soils with high clay content may suffer from poor drainage and aeration, while light (sandy) soils do not retain moisture well and dry out quickly. Soil structure, which refers to the arrangement of soil particles and the formation of aggregates, plays a key role in water infiltration, root aeration, and root penetration. Improving soil structure through biological activity and the addition of organic matter is essential.
  • Organic Matter Content: Organic matter is the backbone of soil fertility. It plays a fundamental role in improving water-holding capacity, increasing soil structural stability, enhancing cation exchange capacity (CEC), and providing nutrients to plants. Soils rich in organic matter create a more suitable environment for barley growth by improving physical and chemical properties, directly increasing 'dryland barley yield per hectare.' The application of organic fertilizers, compost, and green manure, along with crop rotation with legumes, are effective strategies for increasing and maintaining soil organic matter.
  • Soil pH and NutrientsSoil pH (acidity or alkalinity) affects the solubility and uptake of nutrients by plants. The optimal pH for barley growth is usually in the range of 6.0 to 7.5. In highly acidic or alkaline pH, certain nutrients may become less available or even reach toxic levels. Deficiencies or excesses of primary macronutrients such as nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium, as well as micronutrients like zinc, iron, and manganese, can limit barley growth and significantly reduce 'dryland barley yield per hectare'. Regular soil testing and fertilization based on the results are essential to meet plant nutritional needs and correct soil pH if necessary.

3. Selecting an appropriate barley variety: Genetic factor and adaptation

Selecting the appropriate barley variety is one of the most critical decisions in dryland farming that directly impacts 'dryland barley yield per hectare'. This selection must be made carefully and based on local regional conditions.

  • Drought and disease resistanceVarieties that are genetically more drought-resistant and exhibit higher tolerance to water stress are ideal for dryland farming. These varieties generally have deeper root systems, higher water use efficiency, and the ability to resume growth after stress. Additionally, resistance to common regional diseases, such as rusts (yellow, brown, and black rust), powdery mildew, and net blotch of barley, can reduce losses caused by these diseases and maintain yield. Selecting varieties resistant to these diseases also reduces the need for spraying and lowers production costs.
  • Regional adaptationDifferent barley varieties are developed for specific climatic and soil conditions. Selecting a variety adapted to the growing season length, rainfall patterns, prevalent temperatures, and day length in your region is of high importance. Varieties that reach maturity earlier may benefit more from spring rainfall and complete grain filling before the onset of severe heat.
  • Yield potentialWhile high yield potential under ideal conditions is desirable, in dryland farming, yield stability under stressful conditions is equally important. Varieties that deliver acceptable yields in years with low rainfall are safer options for dryland farmers. Researchers and seed production companies are continuously introducing new varieties with improved traits for dryland conditions.

4. Optimal crop management: The art of dryland farming

Proper field management, from land preparation to harvest, plays a decisive role in optimizing 'dryland barley yield per hectare'.

  • Sowing dateAn appropriate sowing date allows barley to make the best use of soil stored moisture and seasonal rainfall. In many dryland regions of Iran, autumn sowing (Chi-re-kar) provides access to winter rainfall, while in areas with very cold winters, spring sowing is preferred. Early spring sowing can lead to greater vegetative growth and a stronger root system, whereas late sowing may expose plants to end-of-season heat stress and reduce 'dryland barley yield per hectare'.
  • Planting densityThe ideal plant density should be such that plants are not too dense to compete for water and light (especially in dryland conditions where water is limiting), nor too sparse to underutilize the available space. Appropriate planting density varies based on variety, soil fertility, and rainfall amount and should be determined in consultation with regional agricultural specialists. High plant density can lead to smaller grain size and increased competition for water.
  • Weed, pest, and disease control: Weeds intensely compete with barley for water, light, and nutrients, significantly reducing the 'rainfed barley yield per hectare.' Proper weed management through plowing, crop rotation, use of plant mulches, and, when necessary, appropriate herbicides is essential. Pests and diseases can also cause irreparable crop damage; their timely and effective control through continuous monitoring and the application of biological or chemical control methods (adhering to safety principles) is critical.
  • Fertilization and Plant Nutrition: Even in rainfed cropping, adequate plant nutrition with essential elements can help increase yield. Nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium are among the most important elements. The timing and method of fertilization should be adjusted according to soil type, soil test results, and plant growth stages. For example, phosphorus fertilizers are usually applied at planting to stimulate root growth and enhance water and nutrient uptake, while nitrogen fertilizers may be applied in split doses aligned with seasonal rainfall to prevent leaching.

The Role of Modern Technologies in Increasing Rainfed Barley Yield: Beyond Traditional Concepts

Despite the rainfed nature of barley cultivation, the adoption of modern technologies can significantly improve the 'rainfed barley yield per hectare.' These technologies play a vital role in optimizing water and nutrient management, reducing stress, and increasing resource efficiency.

1. Using Modern Supplemental Irrigation Systems: A Solution to Deficits

Supplemental irrigation is a modern approach in rainfed agriculture where small amounts of water are applied during critical plant growth stages to minimize drought stress. This method can make a significant difference in the 'rainfed barley yield per hectare' in regions with marginal rainfall.

  • Introducing the Concept of Supplemental Irrigation in Rainfed Fields: In rainfed systems, the primary objective of supplemental irrigation is to compensate for soil moisture deficits during periods when rainfall is insufficient to meet the plant's water requirements. This is of paramount importance during critical stages such as tillering, flowering, and grain filling. During these periods, barley plants have an urgent need for adequate moisture for grain development and quality maintenance; even a small supplemental irrigation event can prevent severe yield loss. Numerous studies worldwide have shown that even small amounts of supplemental irrigation (e.g., an additional 50 to 100 millimeters of water) can lead to significant yield increases in rainfed crops. (Source: Complementary irrigation for improved crop water productivity in dry areas – ScienceDirect) This method allows farmers to invest in rainfed regions with reduced risk.
  • Modern Irrigation Methods: In rainfed fields, due to severe water resource limitations, the use of modern irrigation methods that have high water use efficiency is essential. Drip and tape irrigation systems are highly suitable options because they reduce direct surface evaporation and deliver water directly to the root zone. These methods can provide water to the plant with high precision and in a localized manner. For further insight into these systems and their comparison, you can refer to the comprehensive article on Types of Irrigation Systems which details each method with precision.
  • Impact of Tape Drip on Increasing Water Productivity and Rainfed Barley Yield: As a key component of drip irrigation systems, tape drip can deliver water and nutrients slowly, continuously, and in small volumes directly to the plant root zone. This significantly reduces water loss through evaporation, runoff, and deep percolation, thereby dramatically improving water use efficiency. With this system, even very limited supplemental irrigation can effectively improve the 'rainfed barley yield per hectare,' helping the plant pass through critical stages without drought stress. This precision in irrigation, particularly when rainfall ceases or is insufficient, can be the difference between a successful crop and a failed one.
  • Drip tape discharge rate and its efficiency: When selecting drip tape, attention must be paid to the drip tape discharge rate which is of high importance. This rate must be proportional to factors such as soil type (heavier soils require lower discharge rates, while lighter soils require higher rates), crop water requirements at different growth stages, and planting spacing and plant density. Drip tape with precise emitters and specific spacing enables uniform water distribution across entire crop rows. This uniformity in irrigation promotes homogeneous and consistent crop growth, ultimately contributing significantly to a sustainable increase in "rainfed barley yield per hectare," as all plants have equitable access to water and nutrients.

2. Use of drip tape in rainfed barley cultivation: Practical benefits

As previously mentioned, drip tape as an efficient tool in drip irrigation, offers numerous benefits for increasing "rainfed barley yield per hectare" even in supplementary irrigation for rainfed fields, beyond mere water conservation.

  • Benefits of drip tape irrigation for rainfed barley: Among the most prominent benefits of drip tape irrigation the following can be cited:
    • Significant water consumption savings: By delivering water locally and precisely to the plant root zone, water losses through evaporation, runoff, and deep percolation are drastically reduced. This savings can reach 50 percent or even more compared to conventional methods (such as flood irrigation), which is an extraordinary advantage in water-scarce rainfed regions.
    • Reduced weed growth: Since drip tape only moistens the crop bed and leaves the inter-row spaces dry, weed growth is naturally reduced. This reduces competition between barley plants and weeds for water and nutrients, helping to improve growth and increase "rainfed barley yield per hectare."
    • Reduced incidence of fungal diseases: By preventing wetting of leaves and stems, the ambient humidity around the plant is reduced, creating unfavorable conditions for the growth and spread of many foliar and stem fungal diseases.
    • Possibility of simultaneous fertilization with irrigation (Fertigation): This drip tape capability allows soluble fertilizers to reach the root zone directly with irrigation water. This method increases fertilizer uptake efficiency, reduces waste, and enables precise and timely plant nutrition.
    • Increased uniformity of crop and grain quality: Uniform distribution of water and nutrients leads to uniform plant growth, improved grain size and quality, and ultimately increases market acceptance and "barley dryland yield per hectar".
  • Types of Drip Tape: To select the appropriate drip tape, it is necessary to be familiar with types of drip tape . The two common types of drip tape are co-extruded and laminated. Each has specific characteristics and applications. Laminated drip tape is generally more suitable for sloping land and longer row lengths because it has less pressure drop and provides more uniform water distribution over long distances. In contrast, co-extruded drip tape is more commonly used for flatter land and shorter lengths (such as dryland fields requiring limited irrigation rows) and is often more economical.
  • Appropriate Drip Tape Thickness: Drip tape thickness affects durability, resistance to tearing and puncturing, and service life. For annual crops like dryland barley where the tape is collected after harvest, thinner gauges (such as 175 to 200 microns) are sufficient and more cost-effective. However, if you intend to reuse the tape, or if you are in an area with active rodents or rocky soil, using a thicker gauge (e.g., 250 microns or more) is recommended to prevent physical damage and extend the system's service life.
  • Considerations Before Purchasing Irrigation Drip Tape: Before purchasing, a thorough review of considerations before purchasing irrigation drip tape can help you make an informed and economical choice. Paying attention to factors such as roll length, emitter spacing (which must match barley plant spacing), emitter discharge rate, and raw material quality (which must be resistant to UV and clogging) is crucial. Correct selection of drip tape can directly impact irrigation efficiency and ultimately "barley dryland yield per hectar".

Case Study: Barley Dryland Yield in Different Regions of Iran

"Barley dryland yield per hectar" in Iran shows significant variations due to climatic diversity and geographical conditions. These differences are mainly dependent on annual rainfall amounts and distribution, soil fertility, cultivar type, and level of agricultural management.

  • Statistics and Climate-Based DifferencesGenerally, the "dry barley yield per hectare" in Iran ranges from approximately 800 kg in very arid and low-rainfall areas (such as parts of central and southern provinces) to over 2.5 to 3.5 tons per hectare in regions with adequate rainfall and optimal management (such as western and northwestern provinces). For example, in provinces like East Azerbaijan, Kurdistan, and Kermanshah, which have better rainfall and more fertile soils, the average dry barley yield is higher than in provinces such as Semnan or Kerman. These figures indicate the high potential for increasing yields through improved conditions and proper management. (Source: Annual reports of the Iranian Ministry of Agriculture, Jihad on agricultural crops)
  • Climatic factors and their impact: Areas with moderate rainfall (350 to 450 mm during the growing season) can achieve a "dry barley yield per hectare" between 1.5 and 2.5 tons per hectare, provided that rainfall occurs during sensitive plant growth stages. In areas with rainfall below 300 mm, this figure can drop to less than 1 ton per hectare, posing serious challenges to dryland farming. In these regions, selecting highly drought-tolerant varieties and using supplemental irrigation are essential.
  • Comparison with irrigated wheat yield per hectare: Dry barley, despite its numerous advantages in tolerating harsh conditions, naturally has a lower yield compared to irrigated crops like irrigated wheat. For comparison and more information on this topic, you can refer to the article Irrigated wheat yield per hectare Please refer. While the "dry barley yield per hectare" may reach 3.5 tons in the best case, Irrigated wheat yield per hectare can reach 6 to 8 tons or even more with proper management. This significant difference clearly demonstrates the impact of irrigation on increasing yield potential and underscores the importance of smart strategies for optimizing the "dry barley yield per hectare".
  • Research and Development: In Iran, agricultural research centers continuously work on developing new dry barley varieties with higher drought tolerance and better performance. The goal of this research is to provide varieties that can maintain yield stability under varying climatic conditions and significantly contribute to increasing the "dry barley yield per hectare".

Practical strategies for optimizing dry barley yield per hectare: A comprehensive approach

To increase the "dry barley yield per hectare" under unfavorable climatic conditions and limited water resources, a set of management and technical strategies must be implemented in an integrated and intelligent manner. These strategies not only help improve yield but also ensure the sustainability of dryland farming systems.

  • Effective crop rotation: Implementing appropriate crop rotation, especially with legumes (such as lentils and chickpeas) or other drought-tolerant crops, can significantly improve soil fertility, increase organic matter content, and reduce pressure from specific pests and diseases of barley. Legumes, with their ability to fix atmospheric nitrogen in the soil, reduce the need for nitrogen fertilizers for barley and improve soil structure. This leads to better soil moisture retention and, consequently, an increase in the "dry barley yield per hectare".
  • Residue management and conservation tillage: Retaining residue from the previous crop (such as straw and chaff) on the soil surface is a highly effective strategy in dryland farming. This residue acts as natural mulch, significantly reducing water evaporation from the soil surface, increasing rainwater infiltration into the soil, and preventing soil erosion (wind and water). Conservation tillage methods (such as no-till or reduced-till) also help improve soil moisture and organic matter content by maintaining soil structure, thereby contributing significantly to the sustainable increase of the "dry barley yield per hectare".
  • Use of high-efficiency hybrid and improved varieties: Investing in seeds of new barley varieties developed through breeding programs can play a significant role in increasing yield. These varieties typically exhibit higher drought tolerance, better water and nutrient use efficiency, and higher yield potential under stress conditions. Selecting the appropriate variety for each climate zone, based on recommendations from research organizations, is of particular importance.
  • Smart and precision supplementary irrigation: Even in rain-fed fields, targeted supplementary irrigation can be applied during critical plant growth stages using drip and tape irrigation systems. The use of soil moisture sensors, micro-meteorological stations, and climate forecasts allows farmers to determine the precise timing and amount of water required. This intelligent approach maximizes productivity from minimum available water and can create a significant difference in the 'rain-fed barley yield per hectare' during dry years. For implementing this type of irrigation, the use of quality products such as Tipee tape and nylon hoses provided by companies like AFP Aabgostar Faraparsiyan is of high importance. These equipment not only help in water conservation but also ensure uniform and efficient distribution of water and fertilizer.
  • Comprehensive pest and disease management: Continuous field monitoring for early detection of pests and diseases, and the adoption of Integrated Pest Management (IPM) methods, including biological, agronomic, and where necessary, chemical controls, are essential to prevent irreversible crop damage and maintain the 'rain-fed barley yield per hectare'.
  • Need-based fertilization and precise timing: Conducting soil tests and applying fertilizers based on the actual needs of the plant and soil prevents fertilizer wastage and environmental pollution. Precise timing of fertilization (particularly for nitrogen) must be synchronized with plant growth stages and rainfall patterns to ensure maximum uptake and high efficiency.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

What factors have the greatest impact on dry barley yield?

Rainfall (amount and distribution), soil type and fertility, selection of varieties resistant to drought and diseases, and proper agricultural management including planting date and density.

What is supplementary irrigation, and how does it help improve dryland barley yields?

Supplementary irrigation is the provision of limited water during critical growth stages of dryland crops. By reducing drought stress at vital times, this method improves growth and increases final yield.

What are the benefits of using a drip tape for dryland barley cultivation?

Drip tape helps save water, reduce weed growth, decrease fungal diseases, and enable precise and effective fertilization (fertigation), ultimately increasing the 'yield of dryland barley per hectare.'

How can soil fertility be improved to increase dryland barley harvests?

By increasing soil organic matter through animal manure and compost, implementing crop rotation with legumes, and conducting soil tests for balanced and targeted fertilization.

Do improved varieties of dryland barley affect yield levels?

Yes, improved and hybrid dryland barley varieties generally have higher drought resistance, greater efficiency in water and nutrient use, and higher yield potential under stress conditions, which significantly contribute to increasing the 'yield of dryland barley per hectare.'
