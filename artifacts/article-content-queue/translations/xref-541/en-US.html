Introduction to challenges in surface irrigation for cotton cultivation

As a sensitive commercial crop, cotton requires precise water resource management. In many regions of Iran, conventional methods such as surface irrigation remain the primary means of water supply for cotton farms. However, with increasing water scarcity and rising cultivation costs, these methods face significant challenges. In this article, our goal is to technically examine these challenges and introduce alternative solutions.

Why review traditional irrigation methods

Surface irrigation operates on the flow of water in field furrows and channels, based on slope and soil infiltration. Although this method may have been common in the past due to its ease of implementation, it now faces issues such as uneven water distribution, resource waste, and gradual soil salinization in semi-arid regions. By comparing it with modern methods like modern irrigation methods for agricultural lands, it can be seen why drip line has become a central topic in sustainable development.

Sustainable development in agriculture involves not only crop cultivation but also the selection of irrigation methods, which account for half of crop success. Due to its root sensitivity to water deficit at sensitive stages, cotton relies heavily on precise water distribution. This transforms the challenges of surface irrigation into measurable, manageable challenges.

The most critical technical challenges of surface irrigation in cotton fields

1. Uneven water distribution and crop growth variability

One of the most central problems with surface irrigation is the uneven water depth along the furrows. Surface irrigation does not deliver water uniformly to all plants; plants at the head of the furrow typically receive more water than necessary, while plants at the tail experience water shortage. This variation leads to delayed maturity or even wilting of some cotton plants. In contrast, more precise methods such as band drip irrigation ensure that each root zone receives exactly the amount of water it needs.

2. Increased water consumption and hidden costs

In surface irrigation, up to 40% of the water used is lost due to leakage, evaporation, or losses in the conveyance channels. This means the farmer pays for water that the cotton plant does not effectively utilize. Another hidden cost is the labor required for grading and maintaining the furrows. This labor is significantly reduced in more automated methods such as band drip irrigation.

3. Gradual salinization and soil nutrient depletion

The water required for cotton in surface irrigation infiltrates vertically from top to bottom. In soils with low stability, this process can lead to salt deposition in the upper layers, particularly in areas irrigated with saline water. Over the long term, this reduces soil fertility and increases the cost of soil amendment.

4. Entrepreneurial Limitations in Specific Soil and Water Conditions

Surface irrigation requires level land with a uniform slope. For cotton farms, which are often grown in hilly or uneven regions, implementing surface irrigation without extensive and costly modifications is not feasible. In contrast, precise drip-line systems can be better implemented even in less favorable conditions.

Why does drip irrigation better suit cotton?

  • Direct water delivery to the root zone: Cotton is a rooted crop; drip irrigation delivers water directly to the root bed layers, whereas a significant portion of water evaporates with surface irrigation.
  • Salinity management flexibility: By reducing flow rates and increasing irrigation frequency, salinity buildup can be controlled.
  • Scalable automation: Drip lines can be easily connected to automated valves or soil moisture sensors, enabling smart irrigation management.

Practical tips for addressing surface irrigation challenges in cotton

1. Correct Bed Grading

If you still rely on surface irrigation, you must grade the beds exactly according to engineering specifications. Excessive slope causes water to run off rapidly and results in under-irrigation at the tail end of the beds. Major land reclamation (land leveling) may avoid the need for smaller-scale engineering, but for large cotton farms, extensive slope changes are typically costly.

2. Flow Control and Irrigation Scheduling

Using flow-regulating pumps can improve flow stability. Additionally, scheduling irrigation based on the physiological needs of cotton (budding, flowering, or boll formation stages) is critical. Cotton is more sensitive during the boll formation stage; excess water at this stage leads to fiber rot and reduced quality.

3. Selecting an Alternative Drip System

The best approach is a gradual transition from surface irrigation to precision methods. If your farm's existing infrastructure does not allow for this, you can start with small drip sections of 2 to 3 hectares. To better select the appropriate drip line, it is recommended to study the article on green drip lines in cotton farms; the green type (or sometimes black, depending on the brand) protects against UV degradation in direct cotton sunlight.

4. Care for Cotton Roots in Saline-Forming Soil

Cotton is salt-tolerant, but its tolerance is limited. If surface irrigation has caused soil salinization, apply corrective flushing and leaching. Combining this step with more precise drip-tape irrigation can facilitate the restoration of soil stability.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

Is surface irrigation for cotton as economical as drip-tape?

On small farms with limited water access, surface irrigation may appear more cost-effective for initial use. However, as water costs rise and resources decrease, drip-tape is more economical in the long term due to reducing water consumption by up to 50%.

When is drip-tape most advantageous?

If your cotton is at the flowering or lint-setting stage, drip-tape can increase moisture stability and prevent leaching of elements (such as potassium). Additionally, if your cotton soil is salinizing or has low stability, drip-tape helps dissolve salts at greater depths.

What is the optimal installation depth for drip-tape for cotton?

The recommended depth is between 15 and 20 centimeters from the ground surface. At this depth, the tape is located in the primary root zone of cotton. Shallower installation leads to the tape drying out faster and potential mold formation.

Does drip tape also work for other crops like vegetables or beans?

Yes, drip tape is suitable for many crops. However, for each crop, the flow rate, tape length, and installation depth must vary.
