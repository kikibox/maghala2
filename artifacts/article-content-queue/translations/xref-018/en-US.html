Rationale for Revisiting Traditional Shallot Irrigation Methods

Shallot, as a high-demand export and domestic crop, requires precise management of water and input resources. In many areas in northern and western Iran, the sprinkler irrigation with a mainline lateral system is still utilized. This method appears attractive due to its lower initial requirement for complex infrastructure. However, from an economic perspective, a farm cost analysis reveals that this system has hidden drawbacks that affect total crop profitability over time. Below, relying on agricultural data, we will examine the advantages and disadvantages of this method alongside modern systems, such as drip and tape drip, to enable farmers to make informed decisions.

Initial Costs (Capital) in the Mainline Lateral System

Initial investment in sprinkler irrigation with a mainline lateral system typically includes installing simple nozzles, pressure chambers, and central pumps. These costs appear lower compared to the lateral pipe networks of drip systems. However, the maintenance costs of nozzles, due to high suspended particles and sediment, occupy a significant portion of the annual budget. Analysis of Operating Costs and Energy Consumption

One of the largest cost components in sprinkler irrigation is energy consumption. Generating rain requires high main line pressure so water can reach the desired spray from the nozzles. In contrast, drip line systems operate at lower speeds and lower pressures. Hidden Costs: Evaporation and Losses

Sprinkler irrigation in hot and windy weather leads to significant evaporation. A large portion of water is lost before reaching the roots. This means farmers are effectively paying for water and energy to be wasted into the air. Furthermore, soil unevenness and prevailing winds cause irregular water distribution, leaving some areas under-irrigated and others dry. To compensate for these deficits, total water consumption increases.

Economic comparison with drip and drip-tape systems

Drip and drip-tape systems, although having higher initial capital costs, from a perspective of farm costs In the long term, they offer higher returns. In garlic cultivation, roots are sensitive to waterlogging and salinity. The drip-tape system delivers water directly to the root zone. This reduces water demand, thereby optimizing pumping and water costs. Additionally, reduced surface evaporation and weed growth (due to dry inter-row areas) also lower tillage and herbicide costs.

Return on Investment (ROI) assessment

When investing in new irrigation systems, the ROI must be calculated. Although the cost of purchasing pipes and drip components is high, the 50% reduction in water consumption and lower labor costs typically recoup this investment within two to three harvest seasons. Conversely, the spray method, while having low capital costs, yields low long-term economic returns due to high energy consumption and losses. Paying attention to pre-purchase considerations for drip-tape can prevent costly mistakes in purchasing and installation. For example, selecting the appropriate pipe diameter for the mini-pitch spacing is critical.

Impact on Quality and Selling Price

Chia seed quality is directly correlated with plant health. A plant experiencing adverse weather conditions or moisture stress due to uneven irrigation in spray systems will not produce more uniform seeds. Irregular seeds face price discounts in export markets. Therefore, the costs of suboptimal irrigation are recovered through a reduction in the final product price. This creates a vicious cycle in farm costs that must be broken through proper investment.

Practical tips for reducing costs in the existing system

If you are currently using a sprinkler irrigation system and it cannot be replaced in the short term, you can optimize costs by making minor adjustments:

  • Irrigation scheduling: Restrict irrigation to cool nighttime or early morning hours to reduce evaporation.
  • Levelling land: Laser land levelling ensures uniform water distribution and eliminates excess water consumption.
  • Soil insulation: Using mulch nets or plant residues (mulch) to reduce evaporation and conserve moisture is a low-cost measure with high returns.
  • Pressure monitoring: Installing pressure gauges at the end of lines to monitor pressure drop and adjust the pump prevents energy loss.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

Is rainfed irrigation with runoff recommended for green onion in arid regions?

Generally, no. In dry regions, water availability is limited. Investing in a drip or tape irrigation system that minimizes water consumption is far more economically logical. In these areas, water is usually purchased at high rates or sourced from expensive supplies.

What is the maintenance cost of a center-pivot irrigation system?

Due to the collision of soil particles and sediment with open nozzles, the cost of cleaning and replacing nozzles is high. In the early years, this cost may be negligible, but from the second year onward, maintenance costs increase significantly, making up a large portion of the field-level operating costs .

When is it cost-effective to replace a center-pivot system with a drip/tape system?

If the cost of electricity in your region is sufficient to recover the initial capital investment of a drip/tape system within 2 to 3 years, replacing the system is cost-effective. Typically, a 30 to 40 percent reduction in water and energy costs, along with decreased waste, ensures this return on investment.

Final Conclusion

Management Cost on the Farm Cost-effectiveness requires a look beyond the initial equipment price. Although spray irrigation systems may seem low-cost in terms of initial inputs, they subject the farm to significant long-term costs due to high energy consumption, evaporation losses, increased disease pressure, and reduced crop quality. In contrast, micro-irrigation systems such as drip and micro-spray, despite their higher initial investment, ensure more sustainable profitability by maintaining stable soil moisture and reducing operational costs. Our recommendation to farmers is to conduct a precise economic assessment based on local conditions, real-time onion prices, and energy costs, and to move toward optimizing and upgrading irrigation technology rather than maintaining the status quo. This transition transforms a cost into a smart investment for the future of your cultivation.
