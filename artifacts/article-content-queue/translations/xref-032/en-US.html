Introduction: Why Subsurface Irrigation for Onion?

Onion cultivation is one of the most challenging root crops, requiring precise water resource management due to root sensitivity to drought and waterlogging stress. Traditional overhead sprinkler or furrow irrigation systems often lead to water waste, weed growth, and increased leaf fungal diseases. Subsurface Drip Irrigation (SDI), which places water delivery lines at a specific depth in the soil, directly moistens the root zone while keeping the soil surface dry. This method reduces evaporation from the soil surface and provides space for better root management. However, the success of this system in onion cultivation requires strict adherence to specific hydraulic and soil science prerequisites, which are discussed below.

Soil and Geotechnical Prerequisites

Soil Profile Characteristics

The first and most important prerequisite for implementing subsurface irrigation in onions is a precise understanding of the vertical soil profile. Due to their shallow and unbalanced root systems, onions are dependent on depths less than 30 to 40 centimeters. If the irrigation line is installed at an inappropriate depth (for example, 50 centimeters or more), water availability for the main onion roots will decrease. Conversely, if the line is placed too close to the surface, the risk of physical damage to the pipe during planting and harvesting increases. Therefore, determining the exact depth based on the onion rooting depth in the specific region is critical.

Additionally, soil permeability plays a determining role. In clay soils with low permeability, sub-surface irrigation can cause local saturation and root oxygen deficiency unless irrigation intervals are set very short and precisely. Conversely, in sandy soils, rapid water movement and lateral leakage must be compensated for by increasing irrigation frequency. To ensure optimal performance, studying the subsurface application rate per hectare and water absorption rates on a weight and volume basis is a primary prerequisite for designing the irrigation program.

Hydraulic prerequisites and system design

Selection of branch lines and burial depth

In sub-surface onion irrigation, polyethylene (PE) pipeline lines with a diameter of 16 or 20 mm are typically used, which are buried at a specific depth (usually 15 to 25 cm) during final plowing or using depth subsoilers. The primary technical prerequisite is ensuring pipeline integrity against soil stress pressure during sowing. Lines with higher mass weight and adequate tensile strength must be selected. Additionally, the distance between lines must be based on the target wetting radius. For onions, a distance of 45 to 60 cm between lines is usually appropriate to maintain dry zones between lines for plant stress.

One of the hydraulic challenges is the uniform distribution of pressure along long lines. A design prerequisite involves accurately calculating pipeline pressure loss and designing a set of pressure regulating valves so that the pressure difference between the start and end of the line is less than 10%. Practical tips in the sowing phase and root management

Synchronization of seed sowing and line burial

One of the key advantages of sub-surface irrigation is the ability to perform simultaneous line laying and seeding, referred to as 'Simultaneous Planting.' This practice facilitates the early initiation of irrigation. The operational prerequisite is that the onion seeding system must be modified to prevent the sub-surface pipe from breaking and to place the seed at the optimal rooting depth. When using conventional machinery, a necessary prerequisite is the replacement of seeder discs and precise engineering depth adjustment to prevent damage to the irrigation lines.

To properly manage the critical growth stage of onions, familiarity with onion performance per hectare and the water requirements of each growth stage is vital. During the onion bulbing stage, a gradual reduction in water application is necessary to prevent excessive leaf growth and direct the plant's energy toward the underground stem (bulb). The sub-surface system enables this management through precise flow control, but requires continuous monitoring.

Management and maintenance prerequisites

Spare parts and system flushing

Unlike surface systems where components are always visible and replaceable, access to blockages in a sub-surface system is difficult. Therefore, a fundamental prerequisite is the installation of advanced filtration systems (disc and screen filters) in the mainline. Any failure in filtration can lead to blockage of the sub-surface pipe emitters, which is very costly to troubleshoot. Additionally, regular back-flushing of the lines at specified intervals is a system hygiene prerequisite to flush out sediment and microbial contamination that can cause onion root diseases.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

  1. What is the optimal planting line depth for onions?
    Typically between 15 and 25 centimeters. Greater depths place the water source too far from the main onion roots, while shallower depths increase the risk of pipe breakage during harvest.
  2. Does subsurface irrigation reduce onion yield?
    Yes, if design preconditions and planting depth are adhered to, this method reduces water stress, increases bulb size, and improves crop quality.
  3. What level of filtration is required?
    Subsurface filtration is more sensitive. A disc filter with a mesh rating of 120 microns or higher is recommended to prevent clogging of the outlet holes.
  4. Can a drip tape be used instead of a PE pipe?
    Generally, no. Drip tape has a short lifespan, and in subsurface conditions, it is more susceptible to damage from roots and subterranean animals. PE pipe is better for the durability required in this application.

Conclusion

Implementing subsurface irrigation for onion cultivation is a sensitive engineering project that cannot be accomplished by simply purchasing pipes. Adhering to soil scientific preconditions, performing precise hydraulic calculations, adjusting planting depth with the seed drill, and establishing robust filtration infrastructure are the four main pillars of success for this technology. By increasing water efficiency and reducing surface weeds, this system can significantly reduce production costs and enhance onion quality. Farmers must collect local soil and water data and utilize irrigation technical consultants before starting to prevent system failure during the planting stage. Success in subsurface irrigation for onions results from the precise coordination between water technology and root biology.
