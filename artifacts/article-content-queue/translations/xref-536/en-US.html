Comprehensive Introduction: Soil Erosion Challenges in Marjeh Cultivation and the Necessity of Optimal Irrigation

Marjeh, as one of the high-demand products in export and domestic markets, has high sensitivity to root conditions and water stress. The use of traditional methods such as sprinkler irrigation with runoff may initially appear cheap, but it has adverse consequences such as washing away the fertile layer, salt accumulation at the soil surface, and reduced rooting capacity. In this guide, focusing on runoff management and comparison with modern drip and tape systems, practical solutions are provided to increase sustainability and crop yield. Our goal is to find a balance that has a positive impact on farmers' economics and prevents the destruction of natural resources. This technical framework helps farmers make smarter decisions for this year's and future cultivation by being aware of the hidden risks of traditional methods.

Relative Advantages and Short-Term Challenges of the Runoff Method

  • Low initial setup cost and no need for expensive equipment.
  • Good compatibility with land having slight to moderate slopes.
  • Enables rapid irrigation operations in areas with limited access to energy sources.

Despite these advantages, soil erosion is a hidden enemy. Fine soil particles move downslope with water, depleting the delicate border zone roots of nutrients. To compensate for this deficit, combination with tape irrigation or the creation of physical barriers is essential. A detailed review of tape irrigation maintenance costs shows that while there is an initial cost, it offers faster long-term ROI due to reduced erosion and increased yield. This cost-benefit analysis is critical for farmers concerned about limited budgets.

Technical and Scientific Parameters of Rain Irrigation with Ranshan

Physically, erosion is the process of soil particle transport by surface runoff, which is more significant in light and silty soils. In Ranshan cropping, where roots are primarily located in the top 20 centimeters of soil, the loss of this layer is devastating. Adjusting flow intensity and erosion speed is the key to success in this method and requires high engineering precision.

Precise Evaluation and Management of Surface Runoff

  1. Flow Velocity Measurement: Using a flume meter or even empirical methods, you must keep the flow velocity in channels below 0.8 meters per second. Higher velocities mark the onset of severe erosion.
  2. Bed Slope Control: If the natural ground slope exceeds 5 percent, shorten the irrigation lines or use drip tapes. Steep slopes quickly lose equilibrium.
  3. Protection by living cover: Planting sorghum linearly along contour lines reduces flow velocity by 40 percent and prevents gully erosion.

Practical and actionable solutions for reducing soil erosion

If you are still using erosion methods for any reason, minimize damage with these measures:

  • Creating erosion barriers: Place wooden branches or straw in the flow path to break up velocity. Barrier spacing should be determined according to the soil's infiltration capacity.
  • Using mulching: Applying a layer of rice straw or dry grass on the soil surface keeps soil temperature stable and prevents up to 60% of erosion.
  • Smart irrigation scheduling: Avoid irrigation during heavy rainfall. Using meteorological data to adjust irrigation schedules prevents erosion caused by rain.

The dual combination of erosion control and drip-tape systems ensures yield during dry years. Practical plan for one hectare in four key steps

  1. Topographic surveying and zoning: Divide the land into zones with 3 to 5 percent slopes based on a contour map to facilitate flow management.
  2. Water resource management: Do not direct 30% of the water to sloping areas; seal these points with drip-tape to achieve zero erosion.
  3. Contour farming: Plant on contour lines exactly to eliminate gully erosion.
  4. Post-irrigation management: Use crop cover between rows to increase water infiltration and reduce runoff.

Answers to farmers' frequently asked questions

Does drip irrigation completely eliminate runoff?

Yes, almost entirely. Water is released at a constant, low-energy pressure. In contrast, runoff involves dynamic energy. Therefore, surface erosion is effectively zero in drip irrigation.

What is the cost of the combined system?

The cost depends on the land dimensions. However, considering the maintenance cost of drip irrigation, the dual initial investment is recovered within the first year due to the significant increase in yield. This rapid return on investment is a strong incentive for transitioning to modern technologies.

What is the best time to replace the system?

Before the autumn season. At this time, the roots are stronger and can adapt to the new system. To compare performance, refer to the lavender yield per hectare that operates on similar principles.

Final Summary and Future Outlook

Choosing between sprinkler and drip irrigation for wheat is a strategic decision. Sprinklers are cheaper upfront but costly in the long term. Transitioning to drip is essential, as it reduces water use by 30% and increases yield by 40%. Even when using sprinklers, using crop residue cover is recommended to protect the soil and preserve water and soil resources for future generations.
