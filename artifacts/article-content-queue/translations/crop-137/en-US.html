Introduction: Drip Irrigation and Mung Bean Health

For leafy vegetables such as mung beans, root and soil conditions are decisive factors for resistance against diseases and pest attacks. In drip irrigation, because moisture is delivered locally and in a controlled manner, the final distribution of water and nutrients in the root zone differs significantly from surface irrigation. This condition can prevent the spread of soil-borne fungi and help reduce plant stress by creating better soil integrity. Mung bean is a plant sensitive to excess moisture, and controlled irrigation is the most precise method to maintain moisture balance around the roots.

In the following section, through an analysis of the general incidence of mung bean pests and diseases, we examine the impact of tape and drip systems on the prevention and control of these problems. Practical strategies for pumping, salinity adjustment, and spray timing are also described so that farmers can achieve a higher-quality, healthier crop with minimal risk.

Impact of Drip Irrigation on Pest and Disease Incidence

Drip irrigation alters the behavior of many pests and diseases by changing the moisture conditions in the root environment. One of its most important effects is the reduction of surface soil moisture. In surface irrigation, high moisture in the upper layers can serve as a suitable host for insects and oviposition by curling pests. However, in localized irrigation, moisture reaches the roots directly, and the soil surface remains drier over long distances. This can reduce the incidence of certain soil-dwelling insects and root rust.

On the other hand, drip irrigation can promote the growth of soil-borne fungi by increasing root zone humidity, if not properly managed. Therefore, precise regulation of the drip rate, scheduling of foliar sprays, and selection of appropriate drip tape are critical. If watermelon roots remain constantly wet, the risk of root rot and bacterial diseases increases. To prevent these issues, appropriate cultural practices should be employed, and attention must be paid to the dry-wet cycle of the root zone rather than maintaining continuous moisture.

Common watermelon diseases under drip irrigation conditions

In watermelon cultivation, common diseases such as root rust, soil-borne fungi, and bacterial wilt (root firmness) are directly affected by irrigation. Root rust grows more rapidly in conditions of high soil moisture and moderate temperatures. With drip irrigation, if the application rate is correctly adjusted, moisture accumulation in deeper soil layers can be prevented. Additionally, bacterial diseases are more prevalent when root moisture is excessive. Therefore, one of the main principles of managing these pests under drip irrigation is the precise control of irrigation volume and timing.

Vegetable pests and their behavior under drip irrigation

Insects such as small cutworms, fruit flies, and soil-dwelling midges may exhibit increased activity under specific moisture conditions. Cutworms utilize soil moisture for oviposition. Reducing surface moisture in the upper soil layers creates a less suitable refuge for egg-laying. Consequently, surface moisture management via drip tape systems can help reduce the activity of surface pests. Conversely, pests that feed directly on roots may benefit from high root moisture levels. Thus, balancing adequate irrigation and preventing waterlogging is the most important principle.

Practical tips for reducing pest and disease risk

For effective pest and disease management in mung bean under drip irrigation, adherence to the following guidelines is essential:

  • Drip rate adjustment based on water and temperature conditions: On hot days, reducing the drip rate and increasing the frequency of shorter irrigation cycles helps maintain adequate moisture without causing waterlogging.
  • Use of suitable, non-polluted water: If the water source contains sediment, chemicals, or harmful bacteria, it can exacerbate root diseases. Water must be tested for chemical and microbial quality.
  • Mulching management: Appropriate mulching prevents sediment deposition and clogging of drip lines. Selecting the mulch type based on water quality and soil type is critical.
  • Irrigation scheduling: Frequent and prolonged irrigation creates a continuously moist environment. Instead, shorter irrigation cycles with regular intervals are recommended.
  • Crop rotation: Avoid continuous monoculture in areas with a history of pest infestation. Rotating with other crops helps reduce soil pest populations.

Precision Management of Strip and Drip Irrigation in Mung Bean Cultivation

Selecting the appropriate drip and tape type based on texture and cultivation conditions is one of the most critical factors in reducing pest risk. Tape systems with small emitters and regular spacing allow for uniform moisture distribution. However, if the drip rate is too slow, roots remain in high moisture; if it is too fast, water may reach the surface layers, wetting the soil surface instead of the root zone. Therefore, the drip rate must be precisely calibrated to the crop's requirements.

Expanding the drip system across the entire field, rather than a single point, can enhance root uniformity. Additionally, for clay soils, using emitters with higher flow rates and shorter spacing helps prevent clay sedimentation in drip channels. Pest and Disease Management in Crops Under Drip Irrigation Across Different Seasons

The impact of drip irrigation on the prevalence of crop pests and diseases varies by season. During hot seasons, high temperatures and low humidity result in reduced root activity. Under these conditions, there is a risk of root drying. To compensate, short and frequent applications with high precision are recommended. However, if low moisture is not controlled, plant stress occurs, making roots more susceptible to soil-borne pests.

In cold seasons, mung bean rooting is slower, and the risk of waterlogging increases. Over-irrigation during these periods promotes fungal and bacterial growth in the roots. Therefore, reducing the drip rate and increasing root drying periods are essential during cold months. Additionally, managing root zone moisture in deeper soil layers is crucial to prevent the formation of a permanently wet environment.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

  1. Does drip irrigation help control mung bean root rot?

    Yes, if the drip rate is correctly adjusted and appropriate fertilization is applied, it can prevent excess moisture in soil layers and reduce root rot growth. However, if mung bean rooting is slowed by severe root dryness, susceptibility to root rot increases.

  2. Is the use of fertilizer necessary in the drip irrigation system for mung beans?

    Yes, appropriate fertilizers, especially in drip irrigation with drip lines, prevent sediment deposition and clogging of drip emitters. Selecting the fertilizer type based on water quality and soil type is a key factor in proper system performance.

  3. When should I use repeated spraying?

    Repeated spraying is recommended when water tension is appropriate, temperatures are moderate, and root zone moisture needs to be increased. However, under dry and high-temperature conditions, prolonged spraying can cause waterlogging at the roots and create a favorable environment for fungal growth. It is advisable to use shorter spraying intervals with regular spacing.

Adhering to these principles reduces the risk of fungal and bacterial diseases while preventing root drying and plant stress. Additionally, selecting the suitable type of drip line based on soil water tension plays a significant role in the uniform distribution of moisture.
