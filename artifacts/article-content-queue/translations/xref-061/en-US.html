Introduction: Why Proper Tomato Irrigation Matters?

The tomato plant is highly sensitive to moisture balance. Water stress, whether it is a deficit or excess, can lead to blossom end rot, fruit cracking, and a significant reduction in fruit weight. Unlike many vegetables, this plant has a deep root system that can penetrate up to 50 cm into the soil. This root depth allows the plant to utilize moisture sources deeper in the soil, but this does not mean that smart management is unnecessary. Modern irrigation systems: flood, sprinkler, and drip

Choosing the right irrigation system is the ultimate determinant of your performance. The older flood system, due to its uneven water distribution, is now obsolete in modern greenhouses. Drip irrigation is recognized as the gold standard in tomato cultivation. This system delivers water and nutrients directly to the roots through the soil. Its primary advantage is preventing leaf wetness, which minimizes the risk of leaf-spot fungi and chemical diseases. Additionally, this method allows for more precise control of the soil solution's EC and pH. We recommend using drip lines with 20-centimeter spacings, as this provides greater flexibility. Analysis of plant water requirements at various growth stages

Tomato plants require varying water levels at different growth stages. During the seedling stage, the plant needs high soil moisture to promote rapid stem growth. As flowering begins, water volume should be slightly reduced to shift the plant's focus from vegetative growth to fruit production. This is the most critical stage; excessive water causes flower drop, while insufficient water results in small, poor-quality fruits. During the fruit harvest stage, you must increase water again to boost fruit size. These fluctuations are natural and should be managed using soil moisture sensors. Our primary recommendation is the use of a Tension Sensor (Tension Sensor). This tool allows you to measure root zone soil water tension without digging. If the plant is stressed, you can adjust irrigation accordingly. Fertigation and pH Management

One of the greatest advantages of modern irrigation systems is their ability to deliver nutrients with water. This technique is called Fertigation. You can supply water-soluble fertilizers directly to plant roots through drip irrigation. This promotes faster nutrient uptake and reduces fertilizer waste. However, the critical point is controlling the pH of the water and solution. The pH must be maintained between 5.5 and 6.5 to ensure nutrients like iron and magnesium remain available to the roots. If the pH rises, these minerals precipitate in the soil, causing micronutrient deficiencies in the plant. Symptoms of iron deficiency in tomatoes include yellowing of young leaves while the veins remain green. This is one of the most common issues that can be resolved by correctly adjusting the solution composition and pH. You should check the pH of the soil solution weekly and add pH-adjusting acids as needed. This process allows you to fully control plant growth. The Impact of Moisture on Tomato Quality and Taste

Soil moisture directly affects acidity and fruit sugar. If there is excess water, plant stems become long and thin, and energy for fruit production decreases. The result is waterlogged tomatoes that are flavorless and pale. Conversely, water shortage increases the concentration of solids in the fruit, but simultaneous severe stress causes fruit cracking and flower abortion. A healthy tomato plant must be balanced. To achieve this balance, you must monitor water and nutrient equilibrium. One sign of water stress is leaf rolling during the day. If leaves transition from an open state to a cup-shaped or claw-like state at the peak of the day's heat, it indicates root stress. Do not confuse this with concerns regarding light deficiency. Low light causes stem elongation, whereas water stress causes cupping. You must distinguish between these signs. If your plant has long, green stems with normal leaf twisting, you need not worry, but if cupping is present in the lower leaves, irrigation must be adjusted. A successful crop is achieved when you can visually identify and understand these signs.

Post-harvest management: Storing tomatoes in the pot

Tomato harvesting is not just about picking the fruit from the plant; it is the start of the preservation process. Tomatoes are sensitive to physical damage and temperature changes. The optimal storage temperature is 10 to 12 degrees Celsius with a relative humidity of 90 to 95 percent. If the temperature drops below 12 degrees, the fruit loses flavor. If it exceeds 15 degrees, the rate of decay multiplies. Therefore, the cold chain is critical from the moment of harvest until it reaches the consumer. We recommend placing tomatoes in high-quality packaging that protects them from impact and pressure.
