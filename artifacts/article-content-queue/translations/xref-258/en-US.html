Introduction to the importance of core irrigation in pepper production

Pepper is a moisture-sensitive crop where precise water management plays a decisive role in fruit quality and final yield. Core irrigation for pepper This means targeted water delivery to the root zone rather than saturating the entire soil. This approach not only reduces evaporation and water loss but also encourages roots to grow toward the moisture. In this guide, we compare different methods so you can make decisions based on your farm's conditions. Core irrigation, by creating an alternating moisture pattern, causes lateral roots to form around the water lines and prevents irregular root distribution in inactive depths. This mechanism is vital because, during droughts or extreme heat, plants with active roots in the water-covered zone respond more quickly to water stress and maintain their photosynthetic performance. Therefore, choosing the right core method is the first step toward a sustainable and profitable crop.

Technical analysis of irrigation methods in pepper cultivation

Drip and trickle line irrigation

The most common systems available are drip and trickle line. In the system Selecting the right trickle line Particular attention must be paid to the spacing between tape lines and the emitter discharge rate. For paprika, balancing water infiltration depth with surface spread is critical. Tubular tape systems are generally preferred for open-field or low-space greenhouse crops, as they offer faster installation and lower initial costs compared to individual emitters. However, individual emitters provide greater flexibility in network design, making it easier to relocate plants or adjust field dimensions. When correctly designed, both methods can confine the wet zone to the main root zone. A key design consideration is calculating the discharge rate; if the rate exceeds the soil infiltration capacity, surface runoff occurs, causing leaching of nutrients into soil layers deeper than the root zone. Therefore, the choice between tape and emitters must align with soil texture (sandy, loamy, or clayey) to ensure the discharge rate fully matches the soil infiltration rate.

Comparison with flood and sprinkler irrigation

Although core irrigation has replaced traditional methods, understanding the differences is helpful. Flood irrigation leads to long-term soil salinization and root fungal diseases. Sprinkler irrigation also results in water spraying on paprika leaves, which increases the risk of Downy Mildew and powdery mildew. Therefore, core systems like drip irrigation, which keep the soil surface dry while moistening the roots, are the best choice for maintaining plant health. Practical tips for optimizing central irrigation

For successful implementation of central irrigation in peppersobserving the following points is essential. Since peppers require continuous chemical nutrition, combining irrigation with foliar feeding or fertigation systems doubles the efficiency. Salinity and EC management of the nutrient solution are other major challenges; because the reduced water volume compared to conventional methods increases the risk of salt accumulation around the roots. To compensate for this issue, root leaching cycles must be scheduled so that accumulated salts are flushed out of the soil profile by water flow.

  • Time scheduling: Perform irrigation during the cooler hours of the day (dawn or dusk) to reduce evaporation.
  • Rooting depth: Prevent the horizontal spreading of water into deeper layers; active pepper roots are typically located within the top 40 cm of soil.
  • Salinity monitoring: Since water confined to the root zone does not facilitate salt leaching, a deep soil flushing must be performed every few weeks.
  • Seed quality and planting assessment: The quality of the initial plant material significantly affects the response to irrigation. Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

  1. Is drip irrigation different for greenhouse paprika compared to open-field cultivation? Yes. In greenhouses, air humidity control is more critical, and you may need increased water distribution to manage soil salinity in closed-loop systems. In open fields, deeper percolation is the priority.
  2. How often should paprika be irrigated? This depends on temperature and soil type, but generally 2 to 3 applications per day with short doses are essential to prevent root desiccation. Using soil moisture sensors can provide more precise scheduling.
  3. Can I apply fertilizer solution simultaneously with irrigation? Yes, this process is called Fertigation, and it maximizes nitrogen and potassium uptake efficiency in paprika.
