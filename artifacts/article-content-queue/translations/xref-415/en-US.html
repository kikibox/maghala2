Introduction to the challenges of rain irrigation in vegetable crops

Vegetable cultivation requires precise water resource management due to rapid growth and high root sensitivity to environmental stresses. Rain irrigation with runoff In this method, water is directed toward distant sections through ground channels; despite its operational simplicity, it creates several problems for farmers. The following section introduces the main challenges of this method regarding vegetables. This method, which was conventional in the past, has remained prevalent in many small vegetable farms because it does not require complex equipment and can be performed with access to soil and water. However, with rising labor costs and increased market sensitivity to visual and chemical product quality, this method faces significant pressures that require special attention in farm management.

Increased soil salinity and reduced permeability

One of the major issues with rain irrigation is the accumulation of salts at the soil surface due to high evaporation. In leafy vegetable cultivation, which is often conducted during warm seasons, this leads to reduced root water uptake and decreased plant growth rate. Studies indicate that in soils with low permeability, surface salinity can reduce yield by up to 30%. This cumulative salt process gradually degrades soil structure, and restoring soil chemical balance incurs high costs.

Soil erosion and turbidity

The movement of water from one point to another creates strong surface currents, leading to soil erosion, damage to delicate vegetable seeds, and mud splashing on leaves. This erosion not only removes soil nutrients but also blocks water infiltration to the fine roots of vegetables. Additionally, mud splashing on leaves increases microbial contamination and reduces the visual quality of the produce at harvest, affecting its marketability in wholesale markets.

Impact of Rain Spray Irrigation with Runoff on Vegetable Crop Quality

The final quality of vegetables is strongly affected by uniform moisture distribution. In rain spray irrigation, over-irrigation typically occurs at the beginning of the irrigation furrow and under-irrigation at the end. This unevenness causes plants at the start of the row to be more vibrant, while plants at the end appear paler and smaller. This growth variability eliminates uniformity in the produce and complicates subsequent steps such as washing and packaging.

Risk of Fungal Diseases

  • High relative humidity around leaves due to water droplets spraying toward the plant
  • Severe soil moisture fluctuations that impose physiological stress on the plant
  • Prolonged soil heat during summer and increased activity of soil-borne fungi

To prevent these issues, using resistant cultivars and adhering to proper spacing principles is essential. Practical Challenges in Implementing and Managing Irrigation

Need for Leveling and Appropriate Slope

In wash irrigation, surface slope can be challenging. A steep slope causes water to move too quickly, leaving insufficient time for soil infiltration. Conversely, a shallow slope leads to water pooling and waterlogging of vegetable roots. Establishing an optimal slope for effective wash irrigation requires high precision in field preparation. These settings must be monitored throughout each irrigation cycle to accommodate changes in soil surface conditions.

Labor Costs and Scheduling

Each wash irrigation cycle requires constant supervision to prevent water from escaping the designated channel. This process is time-consuming and demands more labor compared to modern systems. Under high-temperature conditions, frequent night-time irrigation is necessary to reduce evaporation, which complicates scheduling coordination.

Practical solutions to mitigate challenges

If you must use sprinkler irrigation, implementing key measures can reduce the severity of challenges:

  1. Lining channels: Using polyethylene or concrete lining in the erosion path to reduce water seepage into the banks and maintain water volume.
  2. Split irrigation: Dividing furrows into smaller sections and irrigating them sequentially instead of all at once.
  3. Use of organic fertilizers: Increasing soil organic matter to improve permeability and reduce salinity effects.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

Is sprinkler irrigation with runoff suitable for moisture-sensitive vegetables?

No. For moisture-sensitive crops such as cauliflower and lettuce, it is better to use localized irrigation methods to prevent moisture from reaching the leaves directly and to avoid fungal leaf spots.

How can I determine the appropriate slope for water runoff?

You can use a hydraulic hose or a level device to set the slope to a range of 0.5 to 1 percent. If the water flow is too fast, the slope is too steep; if it is too slow, the slope is too flat.

Is it possible to combine sprinkler irrigation with fertilization?

It is possible, but there is a risk of localized salinity and leaf burn. It is better to apply fertilizer using strip or drip methods so the roots can benefit directly without damage.

Summary and final recommendations

Rainfed irrigation, while having a low initial cost, entails multiple risks such as reduced quality, soil erosion, and high labor consumption in high-value vegetable cultivation. For farmers operating in humid regions with permeable soil, this method may still be viable; however, for most dry and semi-dry climates, transitioning to localized irrigation systems is recommended.
