Introduction to Furrow Irrigation

Furrow Irrigation is one of the oldest and still most widely used irrigation methods in the world, primarily employed in row crops such as corn, barley, wheat, and forage. In this method, water flows through small furrows or channels along the crop rows and gradually infiltrates toward the plant bases. Although this method is simpler and cheaper than drip or sprinkler irrigation, maximizing its efficiency requires precise technical knowledge in geology, soil science, and hydraulics. The goal of this comprehensive guide is to examine the various dimensions of furrow irrigation, including the design of water delivery channels, bed slope, and irrigation scheduling, so that farmers can achieve the maximum impact of their water use on plant roots.

The energy required to transmit water in this system is lower than in pressurized systems, but the main challenge is the uniform distribution of water along the entire length of the furrow. Non-uniform water delivery can cause moisture deficiency in one section and waterlogging in another, directly affecting crop yield. Therefore, a deep understanding of water flow dynamics in furrows is the first step for successfully managing this system.

Technical and Hydraulic Principles of Furrow Irrigation

Designing Furrow Slope and Width

The most important parameter in the correct design of furrow irrigation is land slope. Slopes ranging from gentle to 1 to 3 percent provide the ideal conditions for creating a slow and uniform flow. If the slope is steeper, water flow becomes faster, causing soil erosion and uneven infiltration; conversely, a very low slope leads to water stagnation and accumulation. Furrow width must also be adjusted based on the diameter of water delivery pipes and crop row spacing to maintain appropriate flow velocity.

The Role of Soil Infiltrability

Soil type is the primary determinant of furrow irrigation timing. In sandy or light soils, water infiltration rates are high, so the inflow time must be short and the irrigation continuity high to ensure the entire furrow is moistened before complete infiltration. Conversely, in clay or heavy soils, infiltration is much slower and ponding is likely to occur if the flow is continuous. Understanding farm soil types is vital for adjusting irrigation timing. Advantages and Disadvantages of Furrow Irrigation

Furrow irrigation is a method with relatively low initial capital costs and does not require complex equipment. This makes it attractive to a large proportion of farmers with limited budgets. Additionally, it is suitable for farms with access to surface water sources, as it exerts low pressure on water conveyance systems. However, the method has two main drawbacks: first, the reliability of water distribution in long furrows is questionable, leading to water loss at the tail ends; second, it causes instability in water delivery to roots during sensitive growth stages.

Economic Analysis and Efficiency

To improve economic conditions and efficiency in farms using furrow irrigation, precise management of irrigation timing is essential. The loss of excess water in furrows can increase pumping energy costs and water collection expenses. Therefore, it is essential to use loss reduction techniques and smart water resource management. Practical tips for proper installation and execution

  1. Bed preparation: Leveling the ground precisely before creating channels to prevent water accumulation in specific points.
  2. Use of distribution pipes: Instead of hand-digged channels, use PVC pipes or distribution tapes for more precise control of water output at the head of the channels.
  3. Irrigation scheduling: Irrigation during the cooler hours of the day (early morning or evening) is recommended to reduce surface water evaporation.
  4. Monitoring infiltration depth: Use of soil probes (suckers) to monitor the depth of water infiltration to the roots.

One common challenge in implementing furrow irrigation is seepage from the sides of the furrows, which causes drying of plants near the furrow. To overcome this problem, alternating planting techniques or the use of plant cover can be employed to reduce evaporation between furrows. Comparison with modern irrigation methods

Although furrow irrigation is still widespread, more modern methods such as drip tape irrigation are considered a better alternative for some crops due to more uniform distribution and reduced evaporation. Drip tape irrigation allows for more precise control of root zone moisture. The choice between furrow irrigation and modern methods depends on factors such as crop type, climate conditions, and farmer budget. In areas with severe water scarcity and dry climate conditions, the transition to more precise irrigation systems is more essential.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

  • Which crops are most suitable for furrow irrigation?
    This method is suitable for row crops with long rows such as barley, wheat, corn, and some forages. It is not ideal for root crops or flood-sensitive crops such as pistachio and almond.
  • How can water losses in furrow irrigation be reduced?
    Using sand dams for flow control, planting crops on optimal slopes, and irrigating during the cooler hours of the day are among the most important methods.
  • Is furrow irrigation combined with drip tape?
    Yes, in some integrated farms, transfer channels are used to supply water to drip tape systems, eliminating the need for high-pressure pumping.

Conclusion
