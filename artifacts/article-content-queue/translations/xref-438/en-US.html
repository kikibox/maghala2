Introduction: Why Subsurface Irrigation Matters for Cucurbit Crops

Cultivation of cucurbit crops, particularly butternut squash and dried varieties, requires stable soil moisture at depth. In Iran's climate, characterized by water scarcity and thermal stress, adopting modern irrigation methods such as subsurface irrigation can address the most critical challenges faced by farmers. This method delivers water directly to the root zone, preventing significant surface evaporation and placing moisture precisely where the plant needs it. This ensures that deep roots, which are vital for nutrient uptake and plant stability, receive consistent oxygen and moisture. Practically, cucurbits are highly sensitive to high surface humidity due to their stem structure and large leaves; subsurface irrigation significantly reduces fungal diseases such as Botrytis by keeping stems dry. Additionally, maintaining stable temperatures around the roots during hot months prevents growth stagnation and improves final fruit quality. Therefore, adopting this method is not merely a water-saving measure but a long-term management strategy to improve crop quality and yield.

Compared to traditional surface irrigation, subsurface irrigation improves water distribution efficiency and reduces the incidence of fungal diseases typically caused by persistent wetness of leaves and stems. For cucurbits, which require a strong and well-developed root system, this technology offers a competitive advantage. Many farmers have confirmed through field trials that after the first season, improvements in soil health and a reduction in weeds—which thrive on surface water exposure—are quite noticeable. This reduction in competition allows the plant to direct more energy toward fruit production.

Anatomy and Principles of Subsurface Irrigation in Squash Orchards

Mechanism of water delivery through subsurface installations

Subsurface irrigation installations consist of polyethylene lines or drip hoses placed at a specific depth (typically 20 to 30 cm) below the soil surface. This depth is selected based on the primary root zone of squash. The main objective is to create a permanently moist zone in the immediate vicinity of the pipes and roots, allowing nutrients to be delivered alongside water. Water movement in the soil through capillary action and gravity ensures that lateral roots receive sufficient moisture even where pipes are absent. Proper network design, specifically the logical spacing of pipes from squash rows, guarantees that no dry spots remain around the main roots. In light soils, infiltration rates are higher, potentially requiring increased drip emitter flow rates; conversely, in heavy soils, lower flow rates combined with longer irrigation durations are more effective.

Impact on soil structure and oxygen supply

A common concern is the risk of poor drainage and reduced oxygen supply to squash roots. This risk is minimized by selecting pipes with appropriate diaphragms and maintaining adequate field slope. Irrigation with low, regular flow rates helps maintain the air-water balance in the soil pore space and prevents root rot. Additionally, controlled moisture at depth encourages squash roots to grow deeper and spread laterally rather than expanding horizontally to seek water, resulting in a denser root network. This dense root system enhances plant resilience against climatic stresses such as temporary drought or windstorms. On the other hand, reduced surface moisture leads to decreased activity of white grubs and other soil-dwelling pests that thrive in cool, moist surface environments. Therefore, subsurface irrigation management serves as an indirect tool for biological pest control.

Practical tips and installation guide

To achieve optimal yield in cash crops, precision in subsurface installation is essential. Here are the execution steps to ensure the best results in your orchard:

  1. Soil Preparation: The soil in the squash orchard must be cleared of physical contaminants (such as large rocks) to prevent damage to the pipes. Use a protective mesh if necessary. Additionally, shallow plowing is required to break up the compacted layer (plow pan) to improve water infiltration.
  2. Depth Definitions: The burial depth of the pipes is typically 20 centimeters. If the soil is clayey and heavy, increase the depth to 25 to 30 centimeters to better prevent the risk of freezing or surface evaporation. In sandy soils, a shallower depth may be more effective due to the high infiltration rate.
  3. Slitting and Connection: Use high-quality fittings to prevent minor leaks that cause local root rot. Sealing joints with PVC cement or chain connections ensures the stability of the system under pressure.
  4. Pressure management system: Installing a pressure regulator (PRV) at the start of the line is essential to control system pressure to an appropriate level (typically 50 to 60 kg/m²). Excessive pressure causes sub-surface drip line damage, while insufficient pressure leads to improper water dripping.

Decision-making: Surface vs. sub-surface drip installation?

For many farmers, choosing between standard surface drip and sub-surface installation can be difficult. For squash cultivation, if the garden space is small and water access is easy, surface installations (Drip tape) are also operational, but for large-scale crops with sandy and highly porous soils, sub-surface methods are superior. For further study on various methods, you can refer to drip tape installation guidelines, which provide a precise comparison between surface tapes and pressurized pipes. Additionally, it is important to consider maintenance costs for both methods; sub-surface installations are prone to clogging from sediment deposits that require periodic flushing, while surface systems are more vulnerable to pest damage.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

Is subsurface irrigation suitable for all climate zones in Iran?

Yes, but the settings vary. In cold northern regions, pipes should be buried shallower to prevent potential freezing, whereas in hot southern regions, deeper burial helps retain moisture. Additionally, in some very compact clay soils, vertical slits may be required for drainage to prevent root suffocation. The irrigation schedule should be adjusted based on seasonal rainfall; during the rainy season, subsurface irrigation should be stopped or reduced to avoid soil saturation.

Can I combine subsurface and surface drip systems?

Combining both methods in one garden is not recommended due to different pressure management and timing requirements. It is better to select and standardize one primary method. For more information on plant layout, refer to Seed rate per hectare This can be useful in determining installation spacing relative to seeding density. Proper alignment of irrigation lines with cucumber rows ensures that all plants have equal root access to water.

Do subsurface emitters require flushing?

Yes. Due to the lack of direct visual access, descaling of subsurface installations is critical. Use dual-sided flushing pumps to remove calcium and algal deposits from the diaphragms. This should be performed every 2 to 3 months or after heavy federal fertilizer application to prevent sudden clogging.
