Principles of Operation and Engineering of Nodulation Irrigation

Pressure-compensating irrigation is designed on precise hydraulic principles. Unlike simple nozzles, pressure-compensating nozzles feature an internal mechanism (a pressure-compensating diaphragm) that restricts and stabilizes water flow. This mechanism ensures that minor fluctuations in the pressure at the mainline have a negligible impact on the output flow rate of each nozzle. In other words, in critical points per hectare, the use of drip tape, maintaining acceptable pressure drop, and achieving stable flow rates are primary priorities that pressure-compensating irrigation addresses with high precision. This flow stability enables more precise scheduling of irrigation frequency and water consumption. Essentially, the diaphragm functions like an orifice where the pressure drop across it remains constant. Consequently, the output flow rate remains relatively independent of the input pressure. This characteristic ensures that even in long laterals with significant pressure drop, there is no noticeable difference in water distribution between the start and end of the line. This feature is critical for farms with steep slopes where irrigation lines traverse varying elevations. Farmers can eliminate concerns about nozzle performance differences at various field locations and focus on uniform root-zone moisture using this system. Ultimately, this mechanism also indirectly reduces the need for frequent pump pressure adjustments and decreases energy consumption.

The role of the diaphragm in enhancing distribution uniformity

  • Flow Stability: Diaphragms ensure that flow rate differences between the start and end of the lateral line are minimized, even on steep terrain. This stability results in plants throughout the row experiencing uniform growth rates.
  • Resistance to Clogging: The internal design of the emitters ensures that system pressure resistance is less affected by the deposition of fine particles, although precise filtration is still required. This relative tolerance increases the useful life of emitters in water sources with high levels of suspended solids.
  • Suitable for sloped terrain: These systems are the ideal choice for cultivation on sloped land with high elevation differences. On slopes exceeding 5 percent, the use of non-pressure-compensating drip emitters is strongly discouraged because the flow rate at the downhill end will be significantly higher than at the uphill end.

Comparison with other drip and tape strip irrigation methods

To better understand the position of pressure-compensating drip irrigation, it must be compared with other conventional systems. Tape strip systems are generally used for vegetables with dense planting lines. However, when it comes to maintaining stable performance under variable pressure conditions, pressure-compensating systems are superior. This advantage becomes more evident in large farms where pressure drop in main and sub-lines is significant. In conventional systems, this pressure drop causes emitters at the start of the line to deliver more water and those at the end to deliver less, leading to uneven growth and reduced crop quality. On the other hand, the initial cost of pressure-compensating emitters is higher, but considering the reduction in waste, the long-term return on investment is substantial. Additionally, pressure-compensating systems are easier to maintain because emitters require less frequent replacement due to clogging. Practical installation and implementation tips in farms

The correct implementation of node irrigation requires attention to pre-installation, during-installation, and post-installation stages. Below are the most important practical points:

  1. Advanced filtration: Despite the relative resistance of nodes to sediment, the use of disc filters, sand filters, and screen filters (with appropriate mesh size) is mandatory to prevent clogging of the node mechanism. The selection of filter type should be based on the quality of the farm's water source. For example, if well water is used, a sand filter is sufficient, but if irrigation canals are used, disc filters or screen filters are mandatory.
  2. Soil depth guidelines: Soil thickness and type affect emitter installation depth. In sandy soils, emitters should be placed closer to the surface to prevent rapid surface drying. In such cases, factors affecting efficiency, such as the planting method of seedlings under specific soil conditions, become significant; for example, the principles of quantity beet seeds per hectare guide can illustrate general irrigation criteria for root-zone crops. In clay soils, depth should be increased slightly to prevent water accumulation on the surface and fungal growth.
  3. Preparation and distribution of nutrients: One of the main objectives of drip irrigation is precise fertilization (Fertigation). Drip systems allow for precise adjustment of solution concentrations due to flow stability. It must be ensured that fertilizer concentration does not adversely affect system pressure or the potential for precipitation of insoluble materials in the emitters. Therefore, the use of a reliable injection tank system is essential to ensure that fertilizer is not directly introduced into the system in the event of a water supply interruption, which could cause corrosion or blockages.
  4. Check lateral line pressure drop: During initial startup, be sure to measure and record the discharge flow at the beginning and end of the emitter line to confirm the allowable differential. This calculation must be based on the rated operating pressure on the emitters. If the pressure drop exceeds the allowable limit (usually 15-20%), the lines must be shortened or the pipe diameter increased. Also, do not forget periodic flushing using mild acids such as citric acid to remove biofilms. This flushing must be performed in a single pass and without excessive pressure to avoid damaging the emitters.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

Is node irrigation suitable for all slopes?

Yes, one of the biggest advantages of node irrigation is its excellent performance on steep slopes. However, you must ensure that the system operating pressure is adjusted according to the pump capacity and tank height. On very steep slopes, you may need to use nozzles with lower flow rates to prevent water from being sprayed outside the root zone. Also, at the end of sloped lines, pressure drop may be greater; therefore, accurate initial hydraulic calculations are not useless.

Is it possible for the nodes to clog?

Node groups are designed to be more resistant to sediment, but significant clogging occurs when ultra-fine particles (microparticles) or biofilms enter the system. Therefore, filtration and periodic system flushing are essential. Clogging can be detected by observing differences in the time it takes to fill side tanks or a decrease in total system flow rate. If flow reduction is observed, the system must be flushed completely, and nozzles should be replaced with new ones as needed.

What is the difference between node irrigation and conventional drip irrigation?

Conventional drip irrigation is highly sensitive to inlet pressure; if pressure drops, the output flow rate decreases. In contrast, node systems use a flow-stabilizing mechanism that minimizes flow reduction and provides more uniform distribution. This feature ensures that plants throughout the row are nourished consistently without root disparities. In conventional drip systems, it is sometimes observed that plants at the beginning of the row are larger than those at the end, indicating uneven water distribution. However, in node systems, this variation is minimized.
